# Lab 5.3 — Reflection Answers

## Confidence-Based Routing

**Why is a single fixed confidence threshold a reasonable design choice for this lab, versus a more elaborate system?**
A fixed threshold is simple, predictable, and auditable — anyone reviewing
the system can point to exactly one number and know precisely which
findings will be auto-cleared versus escalated. For a regulated domain like
financial compliance, that predictability and auditability is itself
valuable, even though a more elaborate system (per-category thresholds,
calibrated confidence models, etc.) might squeeze out marginal accuracy
gains — simplicity is a legitimate design choice, not just a lab
shortcut.

**What real-world harm does auto-clearing a low-confidence finding actually risk, concretely, in the compliance-review scenario?**
A low-confidence finding that gets auto-cleared without human review means
a potential compliance issue in a quarterly financial report — a missing
disclosure, an unexplained related-party transaction — goes completely
unflagged, with no human ever seeing it. In a regulated context, this
could mean a genuine violation reaches investors or a regulator
unaddressed, which is a far more expensive failure than the "cost" of
routing an ultimately-fine finding to unnecessary human review.

**Why does the threshold apply to the *averaged* confidence of two agreeing passes, rather than either pass's confidence individually?**
Averaging the two independent passes' confidence scores captures something
neither individual score does alone: how strongly *both* assessments agree
that this is a real issue. A finding both passes flagged at 0.7 and 0.8
respectively (average 0.75) reflects two independent lines of reasoning
converging on the same conclusion, which is a stronger signal than either
number alone — this is the same underlying principle as Exercise 3's
confirmed-vs-contested logic, just expressed as a number rather than a
binary agreement/disagreement.

## Provenance

**Why does the system attach the quote from `get_quote(line)` locally, instead of trusting the model to include the exact quote in its own JSON output?**
An LLM can misremember or slightly alter text even when trying to quote
accurately — a paraphrase that's 95% correct is still not the actual text
of the report, and in a compliance context "close to the actual wording"
isn't good enough for something a human or regulator might act on. By
having the model report only a line *number* (a much simpler, less
error-prone thing to get exactly right) and attaching the real quote from
the source data ourselves, the quote is provably accurate by construction —
it literally cannot be a hallucination, because it was never generated by
the model at all.

**What does the reviewer do when the model reports a line number outside the report's actual range, and why is that the right behavior rather than raising an error?**
`get_quote(line)` returns nothing (falsy) for an out-of-range line, and
`review()` explicitly checks for that and simply drops that finding from
the cleaned results rather than crashing. This is the right behavior
because a single malformed finding from the model shouldn't take down the
entire review pass — better to silently discard the one bad finding and
keep the rest of the (valid) findings than to lose everything over one
bad line reference.

**Why does `_parse_json_array()` return an empty list on a parse failure instead of raising an exception?**
This follows the same "return failures as data, not exceptions" principle
introduced back in Week 2's reliable-tools lab: a parse failure here means
this particular review pass produced nothing usable, but that shouldn't
crash the whole pipeline — `main.py` can proceed with whatever the *other*
pass found, or simply report zero findings from this pass, rather than the
entire compliance review failing because one API response happened to be
malformed.

## Confirmed vs. Contested Findings

**Why use two prompts that are "slightly different" (`PROMPT_STRICT` vs `PROMPT_GENERAL`) rather than running the exact same prompt twice?**
Running the identical prompt twice against the same input would likely
produce very similar (or even identical) results most of the time, since
there's no meaningfully different perspective being applied — agreement
between two near-identical questions isn't strong evidence of anything.
Using two genuinely different framings (a strict compliance lens vs. a
broader regulatory-attention lens) means agreement between them reflects
two different ways of looking at the report both converging on the same
issue, which is a much stronger signal of a real problem.

**Why does a finding that only one of the two passes caught go to `contested` rather than simply being trusted at whatever confidence that one pass assigned it?**
A single pass's confidence score reflects only that pass's own internal
certainty — it says nothing about whether an independently-framed second
look would agree. Routing a single-pass finding straight to `human_review`
based on its own stated confidence would be trusting a number the second
pass never got a chance to corroborate or contradict. Marking it
"contested" flags the *disagreement itself* as the noteworthy fact worth a
human's attention, separate from either individual pass's confidence.

**How does this two-pass design connect back to the general theme of "resilient systems" from Lab 5.2, even though this lab is about trust rather than crash recovery?**
Both labs are ultimately about not blindly trusting a single point of
failure. Lab 5.2 doesn't trust a single successful run to mean "nothing
can go wrong later" (hence persisted state and crash recovery); Lab 5.3
doesn't trust a single review pass to mean "this finding is definitely
right" (hence a second, independently-framed pass to cross-check it).
Both designs assume any single component — a process, or a model's single
judgment — can be wrong or interrupted, and build in a second layer of
protection rather than relying on the first attempt being correct.
